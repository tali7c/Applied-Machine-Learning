\section{What is Machine Learning?}
\label{sec:whatisml}
% ============================================================================

\begin{frame}[shrink=8]{The Shape of the Problem}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Programs we \emph{can} write by hand}
        Sort a list. Parse a date. Compute a payroll.\\[3pt]
        We know the rule. We encode the rule. Correct by construction.
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{alertblock}{Programs we \emph{cannot}}
        Is this email spam? Which digit is this? Does this scan show a tumour?\\[3pt]
        We \textbf{recognise} the answer but cannot \textbf{state the rule}.
      \end{alertblock}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \vspace{0.4em}
  \onslide<2->{%
  \begin{center}
  \emph{Try it now:} write a precise rule that tells a handwritten \textbf{3}
  from an \textbf{8}, covering every style in this room.
  \end{center}}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \onslide<3->{%
  \begin{block}{Working idea for this course}
    ML is what you reach for when you have \textbf{examples of the answer} but
    not \textbf{a statement of the rule}.\\[2pt]
    {\footnotesize That is the \emph{supervised} case --- most of this course.
    Two other families relax it; we get there shortly.}
  \end{block}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile,shrink=10]{Two Ways to Build a Spam Filter}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.5\textwidth}
      \textbf{Rule-based}
      \begin{lstlisting}
def classify(subject, sender, bangs):
    if "free money" in subject:
        return "SPAM"
    if sender in blocklist:
        return "SPAM"
    if bangs > 5:
        return "SPAM"
    return "HAM"
      \end{lstlisting}
      \footnotesize
      Monday: works.\\
      Tuesday: they write \texttt{fr33 m0ney}. You edit code.\\
      Wednesday: they pad with normal words. You edit code.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}
      \textbf{Learned}
      \begin{lstlisting}
model.fit(emails, labels)
model.predict(new_email)
      \end{lstlisting}
      \footnotesize
      They adapt. Users press ``spam''. You \textbf{refit}.\\[6pt]
      \onslide<2->{The bottleneck moved off \emph{you}: adaptation became a
      \textbf{data} problem, not a \textbf{programming} problem.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \onslide<3->{%
  \begin{alertblock}{But you gave something up --- what?}
    The rule-based filter can \emph{tell you why it fired}. That is
    \textbf{interpretability}. Unit VI recovers some of it with decision trees.
  \end{alertblock}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[shrink=8]{A Working Definition (Mitchell, 1997)}
  \begin{block}{Definition}
    A computer program is said to \textbf{learn} from experience $E$ with
    respect to a class of tasks $T$ and performance measure $P$, if its
    performance at tasks in $T$, as measured by $P$, improves with experience $E$.
  \end{block}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \centering\footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
    \toprule
     & \textbf{Task $T$} & \textbf{Experience $E$} & \textbf{Performance $P$} \\
    \midrule
    Spam filter  & classify email & labelled inbox     & \onslide<2->{fraction correct} \\
    House prices & predict price  & past sales records & \onslide<3->{mean absolute error, in rupees} \\
    Chess engine & choose a move  & games played       & \onslide<4->{win rate} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \vspace{0.4em}
  \onslide<5->{%
  \begin{alertblock}{$P$ is the hard column}
    ``Fraction correct'' sounds obvious --- until you notice that a filter which
    passes \emph{everything} scores 97\% when only 3\% of mail is spam.
    Most failed ML projects failed here, not at the modelling.
  \end{alertblock}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[shrink=10]{Where ML Sits: AI $\supset$ ML $\supset$ DL}
  \centering
  \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.82, every node/.style={transform shape}]

  \vspace{0.2em}
  \begin{alertblock}{Deep learning is a subset, not an upgrade}
    On tabular data of the size you meet in this course, a gradient-boosted tree
    usually \textbf{matches or beats} a neural network, trains in seconds, and
    needs far less tuning. Choosing the smallest model that solves the problem
    is a skill, not a compromise.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[shrink=8]{Why Now? Most of the Mathematics is Old}
  \begin{itemize}[<+->]
    \item Least squares --- Legendre, \textbf{1805}. \quad Perceptron --- \textbf{1958}. \quad Backprop in use --- \textbf{1980s}.
    \item \textbf{Data.} Transactions, sensors, images and logs are digital and cheap to store. Examples now exist as a by-product of ordinary business.
    \item \textbf{Compute.} Parallel hardware made the arithmetic of Unit III affordable at scale.
    \item \textbf{Algorithms and tooling.} Better optimisers and regularisation --- and libraries that put all of it three lines away.
  \end{itemize}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \onslide<5->{%
  \begin{block}{What this means for you}
    Much of what you will do this semester would have been a PhD project in 1995
    and is now an \texttt{import} statement. So the course spends its time on
    \textbf{judgement} --- which method, which metric, is this result honest ---
    not on implementation.
  \end{block}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[shrink=6]{When \emph{Not} to Use Machine Learning}
  \begin{itemize}[<+->]
    \item \textbf{The rule is known and stable.} Income tax is a published formula. A model that gets it 99\% right is \emph{worse} than arithmetic.
    \item \textbf{No labelled examples}, and no realistic way to get them.
    \item \textbf{Errors are unacceptable} and you cannot bound them. A model gives a rate, not a guarantee.
    \item \textbf{Every decision must be explained} to a regulator, and a simple rule would nearly do.
    \item \textbf{Training data will not resemble deployment.} A model trained on last year's customers predicts last year's customers.
  \end{itemize}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \onslide<6->{%
  \begin{exampleblock}{Discussion}
    A hospital wants to predict which patients will miss an appointment, to
    overbook. \textbf{Which of the five bites hardest?}
  \end{exampleblock}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile,shrink=10]{The Same Thing in Code}
  \begin{lstlisting}
import numpy as np
train = np.array([[1.4,0.2],[1.3,0.2],[4.7,1.4],[4.5,1.5],[6.0,2.5]])
new   = np.array([4.6, 1.4])

d = np.sqrt(((train - new)**2).sum(axis=1))
print(np.round(d, 2))
print("nearest row:", d.argmin())
  \end{lstlisting}
  \begin{outblk}
[3.42 3.51 0.1  0.14 1.78]
nearest row: 2
  \end{outblk}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \begin{block}{Two things worth noticing}
    \small
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item KNN does \textbf{no training}. It stores the data and does all the work at prediction time.
      \item The whole method rests on \textbf{distance} --- remember that when we get to scaling.
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[shrink=8]{Unsupervised and Reinforcement}
  \begin{itemize}[<+->]
    \item \textbf{Clustering} --- group similar records: customer segments, disease subtypes. \hfill (Unit VII; lab 13)
    \item \textbf{Dimensionality reduction} --- fewer features, most of the information. \hfill (Unit IV)
    \item \textbf{Anomaly detection} --- find what does not fit. \hfill (lab experiment 10)
    \item \textbf{Reinforcement} --- an agent acts, receives reward, and is never told the right answer. Consequences are often delayed by many steps.
  \end{itemize}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \onslide<5->{%
  \begin{alertblock}{Unsupervised results are harder to judge}
    With no ground truth there is no accuracy to compute. If a clustering splits
    your customers into four groups, nobody can tell you whether those are the
    \emph{right} four --- there is no right. Unit VII spends real time on this.
  \end{alertblock}}
\end{frame}

  \onslide<7->{%
  \begin{exampleblock}{Row 4 repays thought}
    Fraud \emph{could} be supervised --- if you had a reliable label for every
    past transaction. You do not: confirmed fraud is rare, arrives months late,
    and is biased by whatever detector was already running. A problem that is
    supervised in principle is unsupervised in practice, for
    \textbf{operational} reasons, not mathematical ones.
  \end{exampleblock}}
\end{frame}

\section{The Central Difficulty: Generalisation}
\label{sec:general}
% ============================================================================

\begin{frame}[shrink=6]{A Model With 100\% Training Accuracy}
  \begin{block}{Here it is}
    Store every training example. When asked to predict, look up the answer.
  \end{block}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \onslide<2->{Perfect score. Zero learning. It will fail completely on anything it has not seen.}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  \onslide<3->{%
  \begin{alertblock}{The goal, stated properly}
    The goal is \textbf{never} to fit the data you have. It is to be right about
    data you have \textbf{never seen}. That property is called
    \textbf{generalisation} --- and splits, cross-validation and regularization
    are all machinery for estimating or improving it.
  \end{alertblock}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[shrink=6]{Holding Data Back}
  \begin{center}
  \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{split}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \begin{alertblock}{The test set is not a tuning signal}
    Try twenty models, pick the best test score, and that score is no longer
    honest --- you used the test set to make a choice. Split three ways, tune on
    validation, touch the test set \textbf{once}.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[shrink=10]{Predict First: Which Degree Wins?}
  We fit polynomials of degree 1, 2, 3 and 12 to 18 noisy points and measure
  RMSE on held-out data.
  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{center}\footnotesize
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Model} & \textbf{Training RMSE} & \textbf{Test RMSE}\\
    \midrule
    Degree 1 (straight line) & 1.32 & \onslide<2->{1.60}\\
    Degree 2 (parabola)      & 0.89 & \onslide<2->{1.14}\\
    Degree 3                 & 0.86 & \onslide<2->{\textbf{1.10}}\\
    Degree 12                & \textbf{0.57} & \onslide<3->{\alert{\textbf{40.52}}}\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \onslide<1->{\small\textit{Before the reveal: which row do you expect to have the lowest \textbf{test} error?}}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \onslide<4->{%
  \begin{alertblock}{Read the last row again}
    Degree 12 is the \textbf{best} model by training error and catastrophically
    the worst by test error --- off by 40 where the data spans about 10. Judge on
    training error and you ship the worst option available.
  \end{alertblock}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[shrink=6]{Overfitting, Seen}
  \begin{center}
  \includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{overfitting}
  \end{center}
  \begin{block}{The gap is the diagnostic}
    \small Both errors high, small gap $\Rightarrow$ \textbf{under-fitting}, model too simple.\\
    Tiny training error, large test error $\Rightarrow$ \textbf{over-fitting}, model learned the noise.\\
    You want lowest \emph{test} error --- which is almost never lowest training error.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile,shrink=10]{Cross-Validation: One Split Is Not Enough}
  Iris has 150 rows, so a 25\% test set is 38 flowers. Two flowers changing
  sides moves accuracy by five points.
  \begin{lstlisting}
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score
scores = cross_val_score(model, X, y, cv=10)
print(scores.round(3))
print(f"mean {scores.mean():.3f}  sd {scores.std():.3f}")
  \end{lstlisting}
  \begin{outblk}
[1.    0.933 1.    0.933 0.867 0.933 0.867 1.    1.    1.   ]
mean 0.953  sd 0.052
  \end{outblk}
  \vspace{0.1em}
  \begin{alertblock}{Look at the spread}
    Folds range from \textbf{0.867 to 1.000}. Reporting ``97.4\% accuracy'' from
    one lucky split would be true and misleading. The honest summary is
    $0.953 \pm 0.052$ --- and quoting ``95.33\%'' is false precision.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

X = rng.normal(size=(100, 5000))   # pure noise
y = rng.integers(0, 2, 100)        # coin flips - NOTHING is predictable

# pick the 20 "best" features using every row, then cross-validate
Xsel  = SelectKBest(f_classif, k=20).fit_transform(X, y)
wrong = cross_val_score(KNeighborsClassifier(3), Xsel, y, cv=10).mean()

# selection inside the pipeline, so it happens inside each fold

right = cross_val_score(pipe, X, y, cv=10).mean()
  \end{lstlisting}
  \vspace{0.1em}
  \onslide<1->{\small\textit{The labels are coin flips. What accuracy \textbf{must} an honest model get?}}
  \onslide<2->
  \begin{outblk}
selection before the split : 0.81
selection inside the fold  : 0.50
  \end{outblk}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[shrink=14]{Data Leakage}
  \begin{alertblock}{81\% accuracy on random noise}
    The selector examined all 5000 columns \textbf{including the test rows'
    labels} and kept the twenty that correlated by chance. Those twenty then
    looked predictive during cross-validation --- because the selection had
    already peeked.
  \end{alertblock}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{block}{Definition}
    \textbf{Data leakage} is the use, at training time, of information that
    would not be available at prediction time.
  \end{block}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{block}{The rule that makes it automatic}
    Any step that \textbf{learns something from the data} --- scaling,
    imputation, feature selection, encoding --- must be fitted on the training
    portion only. Put every such step inside a \texttt{Pipeline} and let
    scikit-learn handle the fold boundaries.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[shrink=6]{By Hand: MAE, MSE and RMSE}
  True values $y = (3,\ 1,\ 4)$, predictions $\hat{y} = (2.5,\ 1.5,\ 4.5)$.\\
  Errors: $y - \hat{y} = (0.5,\ -0.5,\ -0.5)$.
  \vspace{0.3em}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
      \onslide<2->{
      \[\text{MAE} = \frac{0.5 + 0.5 + 0.5}{3} = 0.5\]
      \[\text{MSE} = \frac{0.25 + 0.25 + 0.25}{3} = 0.25\]
      \[\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{0.25} = 0.5\]}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.43\textwidth}
      \onslide<3->{%
      \begin{block}{Now change one}
        Set $\hat{y}_1 = 0$, so errors are $(3, -0.5, -0.5)$:\\[3pt]
        MAE $= 1.333$\\
        RMSE $= 1.780$
      \end{block}}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \onslide<4->{%
  \begin{alertblock}{Why they diverge}
    Squaring punishes the one big error far more than the two small ones. That
    asymmetry is exactly why the choice of loss function matters --- and it is
    the whole of Unit II.
  \end{alertblock}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile,shrink=10]{The Same Thing in NumPy}
  \begin{lstlisting}
y    = np.array([3.0, 1.0, 4.0])
yhat = np.array([2.5, 1.5, 4.5])

print("MAE ", np.mean(np.abs(y - yhat)))
print("MSE ", np.mean((y - yhat)**2))
print("RMSE", np.sqrt(np.mean((y - yhat)**2)))
  \end{lstlisting}
  \begin{outblk}
MAE  0.5
MSE  0.25
RMSE 0.5
  \end{outblk}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \begin{block}{You just wrote the first loss function of the course}
    \texttt{np.mean((y - yhat)**2)} is the \textbf{Mean Squared Error}. Every
    optimiser in Unit III exists to make that number smaller. In a sense the
    whole course is an elaboration of this one line.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile,shrink=6]{Predict First: How Much Faster Is Vectorisation?}

for i in range(len(y)):

mse = total / len(y)

\begin{frame}[fragile,shrink=16]{An Entire Workflow in Twelve Lines}
  \begin{lstlisting}
from sklearn.datasets import load_iris
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline

X, y = load_iris(return_X_y=True)
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(
    X, y, test_size=0.25, random_state=0, stratify=y)

model = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=5))
model.fit(X_train, y_train)
print("test accuracy:", model.score(X_test, y_test))

  \texttt{stratify=y} keeps class balance \textbullet\
  \texttt{make\_pipeline} fits the scaler \emph{inside} training only.}

\begin{frame}[shrink=8]{Summary}
  \begin{itemize}[<+->]
    \item ML suits problems where you have \textbf{examples of the answer} but cannot \textbf{state the rule}.
    \item Mitchell's $T$, $P$, $E$ forces you to name the performance measure first. Most failures are $P$ failures.
    \item \textbf{Supervised} (regression, classification), \textbf{unsupervised} (clustering, dim.\ reduction, anomaly), \textbf{reinforcement} (policy from reward).
    \item The goal is never to fit the training data --- it is to \textbf{generalise}. The train--test \textbf{gap} is your diagnostic.
    \item One split on a small dataset is unreliable. Cross-validate and \textbf{quote the spread}.
    \item \textbf{Leakage} gave 81\% on pure noise. Put every fitted step inside a \texttt{Pipeline}.
    \item Distance- and gradient-based methods need \textbf{scaling}. Trees do not.
  \end{itemize}
